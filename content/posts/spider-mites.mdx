Spider mites are the pest you don't see until the damage is done.

By the time the webbing appears, the colony has been feeding
for weeks. A single female lays up to 200 eggs in her lifetime
and the eggs hatch in three days. The math is not in your favor
if you wait for obvious symptoms.

The advantage is that early detection is straightforward once
you know what to look for.

| What you're seeing                                 | What it means                              |
| -------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Tiny yellow or white stippling across leaf surface | Early stage feeding damage                 |
| Fine webbing between leaves and stems              | Established colony, act immediately        |
| Leaves looking dusty or bronze from a distance     | Heavy infestation, significant damage      |
| Stippling only on upper leaf surface               | Spider mites feeding from underneath       |
| Plant near a heating vent declining fast           | Hot dry air accelerates mite reproduction  |
| Damage appearing in summer or during dry spells    | Conditions favor mite population explosion |

## How to Find Them Before the Webbing

Webbing means the infestation is already significant.

The earlier sign is stippling — tiny yellow or white dots
on the upper surface of leaves caused by the mites piercing
individual cells from underneath and draining the contents.
Up close each dot is a destroyed plant cell. From a distance
the leaf looks pale, dusty, or slightly off-color.

To confirm spider mites before the colony is large enough
to web, hold a white sheet of paper under a suspect leaf
and tap the leaf firmly three times. Spider mites fall onto
the paper and move. They are small enough to look like
ground pepper but they walk, which distinguishes them from
dirt or debris.

Check the undersides of leaves specifically. Spider mites
feed and live on the underside, protected from light and
from most overhead sprays. The upper surface shows the
damage but the mites themselves are below.

## Why Hot and Dry Conditions Accelerate Them

Spider mites thrive in exactly the conditions most homes
create in winter.

Heating systems lower humidity and raise temperature.
Below 40% humidity and above 22°C, spider mite reproduction
accelerates dramatically. A population that would take a
month to become problematic in humid conditions becomes
a full infestation in ten days in a dry heated room.

Plants near heating vents are the most vulnerable. Plants
that were fine all summer can develop severe infestations
within two weeks of turning the heat on in autumn. A
[humidifier](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0C2C9NHZW?tag=plantproble07-20) running near your plants
in winter does double duty — it helps the plants and
actively slows mite reproduction.

## Treatment — Water First

Spider mites dehydrate easily.

Before applying any chemical treatment, take the plant to
a sink or shower and rinse the entire plant thoroughly with
a strong stream of water, paying particular attention to
leaf undersides. This physically removes a significant
portion of the active population and their eggs in one step.

Do this outdoors if possible or over a drain. Mites knocked
off the plant will reinfest it if they land back in the pot.
Let the plant dry before moving it back to its spot.

Repeat this rinse every three days for two weeks. For mild
early-stage infestations caught quickly, water alone is
sometimes enough.

## Treatment — Neem Oil

[Neem oil](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B007CRG4CW?tag=plantproble07-20) disrupts the mite's ability
to feed and reproduce and provides residual protection
after application.

Mix according to package directions and spray the entire
plant with emphasis on leaf undersides. Apply in the
evening rather than in direct light — neem oil can cause
minor leaf burn under intense light before it dries.

Apply once a week for four weeks. Spider mite eggs hatch
in three days and nymphs reach reproductive maturity in
five to seven days. Weekly application breaks the cycle
by killing each new generation before it reproduces.

## Treatment — Insecticidal Soap

[Insecticidal soap](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0BDVCCKKT?tag=plantproble07-20) kills on contact
by disrupting the mite's outer membrane. It has no
residual effect once dry, which means it needs to
coat the mites directly to work.

This makes thorough coverage of leaf undersides essential.
A cursory spray of the top surface does almost nothing.
Apply every four days and alternate with neem oil for
the most complete coverage of both active mites and
the reproductive cycle.

Do not use dish soap as a substitute. The concentration
and pH of dish soap is not calibrated for plant application
and causes leaf burn at the concentrations needed to
affect mites.

## What To Do Right Now

- Do the paper test on any plant showing pale stippling
  or off-color leaves today. Tap three times over white
  paper and look for movement
- Check leaf undersides on every plant near the affected
  one. Spider mites spread through air currents and
  through leaves touching
- Rinse the affected plant in the shower immediately,
  focusing water pressure on leaf undersides
- Move any affected plant away from heating vents and
  raise the humidity in the room
- Start the four-week treatment cycle today and set
  reminders. Missing applications allows the population
  to recover between treatments
