Spider plant babies are the most forgiving thing you can propagate.
They root in water in a week. They survive neglect. They multiply
faster than you can find pots for them.

And yet people lose them constantly in the first two weeks after
planting. Always the same reason.

| What you're seeing                               | What it means                             |
| ------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------- |
| Baby shriveling right after planting in soil     | Planted before roots were long enough     |
| Baby rooted but not growing new leaves           | Normal — wait four weeks                  |
| Roots formed in water but rotting after planting | Too much moisture in the soil             |
| Baby producing its own runners immediately       | Healthy and established                   |
| Leaves turning yellow after transplant           | Transplant stress, usually resolves alone |
| No roots after two weeks in water                | Node not submerged or water too stagnant  |

## Picking the Right Babies

Not every baby is worth propagating.

The ones that root fastest and establish strongest are the ones
with visible aerial root nubs already forming at the base. These
are the small brown bumps at the point where the runner attaches
to the baby. You don't need them to propagate successfully, but
if you see them the plant is already trying to root on its own
and will do it faster in water.

Size matters. Babies smaller than five centimeters tend to be
too undeveloped to root reliably. Anything between five and ten
centimeters is the sweet spot. Larger than that and they
propagate fine, but you're giving away more of the mother plant
than you need to.

## Water Propagation

This is the method that works consistently and lets you see
exactly when roots are ready.

Cut the baby from the runner at the base. Place it in a small
glass or jar so the base and any root nubs are submerged about
two centimeters. The leaves should stay dry above the waterline.
A [small propagation station](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0BTTJNR18?tag=plantproble07-20) holds multiple
babies upright without the leaves touching the water, which
prevents rot.

Change the water every four to five days. Stagnant water
develops bacteria that slow rooting and can rot the base before
roots form. No fertilizer in the water — it encourages algae
and doesn't speed up rooting.

Roots appear in five to ten days in a warm room with indirect
light. Wait until the roots are at least three centimeters long
before planting. This is where most people fail. Two centimeters
looks like enough. It isn't. Short roots don't anchor properly
in soil and the baby dries out before it can establish.

## Planting in Soil

Use a light, well-draining mix. A standard potting mix with
extra perlite added works well. Pure potting mix holds too
much moisture for a baby that just came out of water and is
adjusting to a completely different environment.

A [small pot with drainage](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07VXWGH1S?tag=plantproble07-20) in the eight to
ten centimeter range is the right size. Too large a pot means
too much soil staying wet around roots that can't yet use it.

Plant the baby up to the base of the leaves and firm the soil
gently around the roots. Water thoroughly once, then let the
top two centimeters dry before watering again. This is the
critical window. The baby needs moisture to establish but not
the constant dampness it had in water. Most losses happen here
because people water too frequently in the first two weeks,
trying to help.

Keep it out of direct sun for the first week. Bright indirect
light is enough while the roots adjust to soil.

## Keeping It on the Mother Plant Until It Roots

This is the slower method but produces stronger plants.

Instead of cutting the baby off, pin the runner down into a
small pot of soil placed next to the mother plant. Use a
paperclip bent into a U shape to hold the base of the baby
against the soil surface. Water the small pot normally and wait.

The baby roots while still receiving nutrients from the mother.
Once you see new growth appearing from the center of the baby,
the roots are established. Cut the runner at that point. The
baby is now independent and transplant shock is essentially
eliminated.

This takes three to four weeks longer than water propagation
but produces a plant that is significantly more robust from
the start. Worth it if you're not in a hurry.

## What To Do Right Now

- Check any babies currently growing on your spider plant for
  root nubs at the base — those are ready to propagate today
- If you're propagating in water, measure the roots before
  planting — three centimeters minimum, not two
- If you planted a baby recently and it's shriveling, check
  whether the soil is staying wet. If it is, reduce watering
  immediately and add perlite to the mix if you repot
- Pin a baby down into soil next to the mother plant if you
  want the most reliable result with the least intervention
