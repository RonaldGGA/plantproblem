Mealybugs look harmless. Small, white, cottony, barely moving.
They are not harmless.

A mealybug infestation that goes untreated for three weeks
spreads to every plant within reach. The adults are easy to
kill. The eggs hidden in every crevice of the plant are not.
That's the part most guides skip.

| What you're seeing                                   | What it means                               |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| White cottony clusters at leaf joints and stem bases | Active mealybug infestation                 |
| Sticky residue on leaves below the infestation       | Honeydew from feeding, act immediately      |
| Black sooty mold growing on leaves                   | Fungal growth on honeydew, advanced stage   |
| White fluff on roots when you unpot                  | Root mealybugs, different treatment needed  |
| Infestation returning two weeks after treatment      | Eggs survived, repeat treatment immediately |
| New plants arriving with white fluff at joints       | Quarantine that plant before it spreads     |

## Why They're Hard to Eliminate

Mealybugs reproduce fast and hide well.

A single female lays up to 600 eggs in a cottony sac tucked
into stem joints, leaf undersides, and soil surface. The eggs
are protected by a waxy coating that repels most sprays. The
nymphs that hatch are small enough to hide in any crevice and
mobile enough to move to adjacent plants before you notice them.

Killing the visible adults is straightforward. Killing the
eggs and nymphs requires a different approach and consistent
follow-through over three to four weeks.

## Step 1 — Isolate the Plant Immediately

Move any infested plant away from all other plants before
doing anything else.

Mealybugs spread through direct contact between leaves and
through crawling across surfaces. A plant touching another
plant is actively transferring the infestation while you
read this. One meter of clearance between the infested plant
and everything else is the minimum.

Check every plant that was near the infested one. Look at
stem joints, leaf undersides, and the base of each leaf where
it meets the stem. Mealybugs prefer these protected spots
over open leaf surfaces. Use a magnifying glass if you have
one — early-stage nymphs are nearly transparent.

## Step 2 — Manual Removal

Remove every visible mealybug and egg sac by hand before
applying any treatment.

Dip a cotton swab in [70% isopropyl alcohol](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07NFVQCJP?tag=plantproble07-20)
and press it directly onto each mealybug and cottony cluster.
The alcohol dissolves the waxy protective coating and kills
on contact. Work methodically from the top of the plant down,
checking every joint and the underside of every leaf.

This step is not optional. Spraying over a heavy infestation
without manual removal first means the treatment has to
penetrate through layers of wax and egg sacs. The alcohol
swab gets direct contact that sprays cannot.

Dispose of the cotton swabs in a sealed bag, not in an open
bin near your plants.

## Step 3 — Alcohol Spray

Mix [70% isopropyl alcohol](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07NFVQCJP?tag=plantproble07-20) with water at
a 1:1 ratio in a spray bottle. Add a few drops of dish soap
to help the solution spread across waxy surfaces.

Spray the entire plant thoroughly, covering stems, leaf
undersides, joints, and the soil surface. The alcohol kills
on contact but evaporates quickly and leaves no residue that
harms the plant at this dilution.

Test on one leaf and wait 24 hours before spraying the whole
plant if you haven't used alcohol spray on this plant before.
Some plants, particularly succulents with thin protective
coatings, can show sensitivity.

Repeat every four days for three weeks. The interval matters
because eggs hatch in four to seven days. Spraying every
four days means each new generation of nymphs is killed
before it reaches reproductive maturity.

## Step 4 — Neem Oil Follow-Up

After the first week of alcohol treatment, add
[neem oil](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B007CRG4CW?tag=plantproble07-20) to the rotation.

Neem oil works differently from alcohol. Where alcohol kills
on contact, neem oil is a systemic disruptor — it interferes
with the mealybug's ability to feed and reproduce over time.
Applying both gives you immediate kill from alcohol and
residual protection from neem.

Mix neem oil according to package directions and spray the
entire plant including soil surface once a week. Alternate
with the alcohol spray rather than applying both on the
same day. Neem oil smells strong and breaks down within a
week, so reapplication is necessary to maintain the effect.

## Root Mealybugs

If you see white cottony material on the roots when you
remove the plant from its pot, the infestation is underground.

Root mealybugs are harder to treat because most sprays
can't reach them effectively. The approach is to unpot
the plant completely, wash the roots thoroughly under
running water to remove as many bugs and egg sacs as
possible, treat the clean roots with a diluted neem oil
drench, and repot in completely fresh soil. The old soil
is discarded entirely — it contains eggs that will hatch
regardless of treatment.

## What To Do Right Now

- Move the infested plant away from all other plants before
  touching it, checking it, or treating it
- Check every plant that was near it. Look at stem joints
  and leaf undersides specifically
- Start with the alcohol swab on every visible mealybug
  today, before ordering anything else
- Set a reminder to retreat every four days for the next
  three weeks. Missing one application resets the cycle
- Quarantine any new plants for two weeks before placing
  them near your existing collection
